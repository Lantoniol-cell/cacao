Factor families

WarningTo be read as exploratory

The factor model does not beat the benchmark on any target. The protocol, set before the results, then forbids presenting a ranking of the families as a result. This page therefore describes what a model uses when it does no better than a model without factors. It also shows why the two impact measures do not tell the same story.

Show the code 
import pandas as pd
import plotly.graph_objects as go
from _common_en import DIVERGENT, SEQUENTIEL, SERIE, FAMILIES, lire, tableau

COLONNES = [("sens", 1), ("sens", 5), ("sens", 20), ("sens", 60), ("sens", 120), ("vol", 5), ("vol", 20), ("vol", 60), ("vol", 120)]
ETIQ = [f"{'Dir.' if c == 'sens' else 'Vol.'} {h}" for c, h in COLONNES]


def carte(t, valeur, titre, echelle, centre, texte):
    m = t.pivot_table(index="famille", columns=["cible", "horizon"], values=valeur).reindex(columns=COLONNES)
    m = m.reindex(list(FAMILIES))
    z = m.to_numpy()
    lim = abs(z).max() if centre else None
    fig = go.Figure(go.Heatmap(
        z=z, x=ETIQ, y=[FAMILIES[f] for f in m.index], colorscale=echelle, zmid=0 if centre else None,
        zmin=-lim if centre else 0, zmax=lim if centre else None, xgap=2, ygap=2,
        text=[[texte(v) for v in ligne] for ligne in z], texttemplate="%{text}", textfont=dict(size=11),
        hovertemplate="%{y}<br>%{x}<br>%{text}<extra></extra>", colorbar=dict(thickness=10)))
    fig.update_layout(title=titre, height=430, yaxis=dict(autorange="reversed", automargin=True), xaxis=dict(side="top"),
                      margin=dict(t=110, l=170))
    return fig

The 8 factor families

Each family groups variables that tell the same story about the price of cocoa. The model never receives raw levels, such as a price or a rate. It receives changes (over 1, 3 or 6 months depending on the horizon) or deviations from normal, and each data point enters only once published.

NoteClimate: the weather in cocoa areas

Why. The harvest depends on rainfall, heat and humidity. A bad season can reduce supply and push the price up.

Variables, for Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Indonesia and a world index weighted by production:

  • rainfall over the last 7 and 30 days, deviation from normal (ERA5);
  • rainfall over the last 1, 3 and 6 months, deviation from normal (CHIRPS);
  • maximum temperature, air humidity, deep soil moisture, sunshine and wind, as a 30-day deviation from normal (NASA POWER);
  • El Niño index (ONI) and its change over 3 months.

Weather points are placed in growing areas using the MapSPAM map.

NoteGeopolitics: tensions and conflicts

Why. A political crisis in a producing country can block exports. In 2011, Alassane Ouattara called for the suspension of Ivorian exports during the post-election crisis.

Variables:

  • for Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Indonesia: the country’s share of world news, the share of conflict events and the share of protests, over 30 days (GDELT event database);
  • global geopolitical risk index (GPR): overall level, acts and threats, as a moving average;
  • monthly GPR index by country, for Brazil, Indonesia and Peru. It does not exist for Côte d’Ivoire or Ghana.
NoteBiodiversity: soils, forests and inputs

Why. Soil depletion, deforestation and the use of fertilisers or pesticides weigh on yields in the medium term.

Variables, for Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Indonesia:

  • vegetation fires detected by satellite in cocoa areas, 30-day deviation from normal (NASA FIRMS);
  • pesticide and nitrogen use per cultivated hectare, annual change (FAOSTAT);
  • cocoa yield per hectare, annual change (FAOSTAT). Often estimated by FAO, see the Weather and production page.

FAOSTAT data for a given year are only used from 1 January of year N+2, their approximate publication date. Diseases (swollen shoot) and illegal gold mining have no public series: they are missing.

NoteProduction costs: energy, fertilisers, currencies

Why. Producing, transporting and exporting cost more when energy or fertilisers rise. The currencies of producing countries also change what farmers and exporters earn.

Variables:

  • oil (WTI) and natural gas, as changes;
  • world prices of urea, DAP, TSP and potash, change over 1 and 3 months (World Bank);
  • exchange rates against the dollar of the euro, the Ghanaian cedi, the Indonesian rupiah and the Nigerian naira, as changes. Côte d’Ivoire uses the CFA franc, pegged to the euro.
NoteProduction and supply: the quantities harvested

Why. It is the most direct fundamental: less cocoa harvested should mean a higher price.

Variables: bean production in Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana and Indonesia, annual change (FAOSTAT), available at N+2. It is the smallest family. Stocks data are missing: ICE certified stocks are paid.

NoteMarket and finance: cocoa itself and its context

Why. The recent past of the price (trend, turbulence, volume traded) and the financial context (dollar, interest rates) influence the decisions of buyers and speculators.

Variables:

  • past cocoa return over 1 and 3 months, recent volatility and volume traded, as a deviation from its usual level;
  • dollar index, as a change;
  • US 10-year and 3-month rates, as changes.

This is the information that models without factors already exploit.

NoteInvestors and biases: speculation and attention

Why. Funds can amplify moves when they buy or sell en masse. Strong public or media attention can accompany excesses.

Variables:

  • net positions of funds, non-commercials and producers, as a share of open interest, and their changes. Open interest as a change (weekly CFTC reports, used after their Friday publication);
  • Google searches for “chocolate”, “cocoa” and “cocoa price”, change over 1 month and over 1 year;
  • volume and tone of news articles on cocoa, worldwide and in West Africa, as a moving average (GDELT).
NoteDemand and substitutes: buyers and related products

Why. Chocolate makers are the final buyers. Sugar, palm oil and coffee are grown in the same regions or go into the same products.

Variables:

  • share prices of Barry Callebaut, Hershey, Mondelez, Nestlé and Lindt (two securities), as returns;
  • world prices of sugar, palm oil and robusta coffee, change over 1 and 3 months (World Bank).

Main measure: removing a family

The model is re-estimated without one family, then the loss of out-of-sample performance is measured.

  • Positive (red): the family was helping the model.
  • Negative (blue): the model is better without it.
  • An asterisk marks a 99.4% interval that excludes zero.
Show the code 
r = lire("retrait_familles.csv")
r["sig"] = (r["ic_bas"] > 0) | (r["ic_haut"] < 0)
r["texte"] = r["perte"].round(3).astype(str) + r["sig"].map({True: "*", False: ""})
m = r.set_index(["famille", "cible", "horizon"])["texte"]
fig = carte(r, "perte", "Loss when each family is removed (accuracy for direction, RMSE for volatility)",
            DIVERGENT, True, lambda v: f"{v:+.3f}")
fig.data[0].text = [[m.get((f, c, h), "") for c, h in COLONNES] for f in FAMILIES]
fig.show()
  • Volatility: removing market and finance clearly worsens the model. The useful information lies in past prices and volatility, which the persistence benchmark already captures.
  • Direction at 60 sessions: removing climate improves the model by 6 points. Climate variables add noise there.

Secondary measure: SHAP

Share of each family in the mean absolute SHAP contributions, over 2010-2023.

carte(s, "part", "Share of SHAP contributions by family, 2010-2023", SEQUENTIEL, False, lambda v: f"{v:.0%}").show()

Why the two measures diverge

SHAP gives climate 18 to 27% of contributions for the price direction. The removal test shows, however, that these contributions do not help out of sample. Three reasons:

  1. SHAP measures use, not usefulness. It says which variables the model relies on, not whether that reliance improves the forecast.
  2. Families differ in size. Climate has more than 40 variables. A sum of contributions favours large families.
  3. Families are correlated. The model can spread the same signal across several families.

This is the disagreement anticipated in point 7 of the protocol, and a good methodological lesson: a large SHAP share does not prove that a variable is useful.
