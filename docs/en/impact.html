Impact of weather on production

NoteA complementary, exploratory analysis

The main question of the project concerns the price. This page asks another question: does the weather affect the cocoa harvest? The rules were written in the protocol (point 12) before any computation.

Why this question

The project showed that the weather does not help forecast the price: the market seems to absorb the information before we do. But a cocoa tree does not read the markets. If rainfall and heat matter, their effect should show directly in the quantities harvested. And since the weather does not depend on production, a measured link between the two can be read, with caution, as a cause-and-effect relationship.

The data

  • Production: tonnes of beans per country and per year, from 1991 to 2024, in the 9 countries tracked (FAOSTAT).
  • Rainfall: annual CHIRPS total in each country’s cocoa areas, with points weighted by the MapSPAM crop map, from 1991 to 2025.
  • Temperature: annual mean of daily maximums (NASA POWER), only since 2005.

Why production and not yield? Yield per hectare seemed the natural target. But in Côte d’Ivoire, FAO estimates it itself in 29 years out of 34: it stays frozen around 500 kg/ha. It therefore contains almost no information about the weather. Production, on the other hand, is official in 9 cases out of 10.

The method, in plain terms

Each country is compared with itself:

  • does a year drier or wetter than usual in that country give a harvest below that country’s trend?
  • shocks that hit all countries in the same year, such as world demand or prices, are removed;
  • each country’s long-term growth, linked to the expansion of plantations, is removed as well.

Rainfall is measured as deviations from the country’s normal: 0 is a normal year, −1.5 a clearly dry year, +1.5 a clearly wet year. Rainfall in the year and in the previous year are both tested, because harvest calendars do not follow the calendar year. A “squared” term captures the possibility that too much water may be as harmful as too little.

With only 9 countries, the usual tests are too optimistic. We therefore use:

  • a wild bootstrap: the estimate is redone by randomly flipping the sign of each country’s deviations, over the 512 possible combinations;
  • a permutation: weather years are shuffled within each country, 2,000 times, to see whether the true link does better than chance.

  • Placebo: rainfall in the following year does not “explain” the harvest (p = 0.74 and 0.36). This is expected, and reassuring about the method.
  • The only near signal is rainfall in the previous year, squared (p between 0.05 and 0.07 depending on the variant): a very dry or very wet previous year would go with a slightly larger harvest. It is not significant at 5%, and the direction has no simple agronomic explanation. We do not interpret it.

Why a null result, and what it would take to go further

A null result does not prove that the weather has no effect. It says that these data do not allow it to be measured. Four likely reasons:

  1. Annual, national rainfall is too coarse. What matters for the cocoa tree is how rain is spread through the year, in particular the length of the dry season, not the total.
  2. FAOSTAT production is imperfect. It is often revised and sometimes taken from other bodies. It does not follow the harvest season, which runs from October to September.
  3. Few independent observations: 9 countries, about 33 years.
  4. Other factors dominate: diseases (swollen shoot), ageing orchards, prices paid to farmers, smuggling between neighbouring countries.

Possible extensions: seasonal indicators (dry-season rainfall, number of deficit days), production data by region or by season (bean arrivals at Ivorian ports, ICCO quarterly figures), and extreme temperatures rather than averages. These analyses would be decided after this first result: they would have to be presented as exploratory.
