Which factor weighs most on the price of cocoa?

Forecasting the direction and volatility of the cocoa futures contract (CC=F), 2005-2026

Background

Cocoa is grown mainly in West Africa: among the 9 countries tracked here, Côte d’Ivoire and Ghana account for about half of production (FAOSTAT). It trades on futures markets in New York and London. Its price therefore depends on the weather in growing areas, tree diseases, the policies of producing countries, costs, demand from chocolate makers and investors’ bets.

The question

In 2024 cocoa went through a historic crisis: the futures contract passed 10,000 dollars per tonne for the first time on 26 March 2024 (Bloomberg). This project asks which family of factors brings the most information for forecasting cocoa, out of sample:

  • the direction of the price over 1, 5, 20, 60 and 120 sessions;
  • its volatility over 5, 20, 60 and 120 sessions.

Eight families are tested: climate, geopolitics, biodiversity, production costs, production and supply, market and finance, investors and biases, demand and substitutes.

The short answer

ImportantNo factor family beats a model without factors.

Over 2010-2026, with chronological validation, the factor model (Gradient Boosting) does better than the best model without factors on none of the 9 targets. The protocol rule, set before the results, applies: no ranking of the families is presented as a result.

This result holds with another rainfall source, without the 2024-2026 crisis, with another model, and even with perfect weather for the week ahead (Results page).

Takeaways

  1. The past price already contains the information. What public factors say is already in the price at the time of the forecast. The market seems to absorb available information quickly.
  2. Volatility is predictable, but from its own past. Turbulent periods last. A model that only looks at past volatility (HAR) beats the factor model at every horizon.
  3. SHAP can mislead. It attributes 18 to 27% of contributions to climate for the price direction. Yet removing climate does not worsen the forecasts, and even improves them at 60 sessions (Families page).
  4. A single lead, exploratory: in crisis periods, the volume of news articles on cocoa helps the model forecast volatility at 60 sessions. The model nonetheless remains worse than past volatility alone (Exploratory leads page).
